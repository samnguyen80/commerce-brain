# Reverse Logistics: How Poor Returns Management Drains Your Profit

 

 
 

 
 For most eCommerce brands, returns are simply an accepted part of doing business. Industry averages suggest that anywhere from 15% to 30% of online purchases are eventually sent back.
 Because returns are inevitable, many operators treat them purely as an unavoidable expense categorised under customer service. However, the true financial drag of a return often isn&#x27;t the refund itself, but the operational friction of what happens after the item arrives back at the warehouse.
 If your reverse logistics process is slow or highly manual, you are likely dealing with unprocessed inventory sitting in corners for weeks. Here is a look at why this delay drains your profit margins, and how scaling brands optimise their workflows to recover capital faster.

## The Cost of the Returned Goods 
 When a customer returns a product, two things usually happen: the item arrives at your receiving dock, and the customer’s refund is processed.
 At this moment, your business is at a financial disadvantage. You have returned the cash, but you do not yet have a product ready for resale to offset that loss.
 In many warehouses, returns are treated as a low-priority task. They are pushed to the side during busy picking hours, resulting in a growing backlog of unprocessed boxes. Every day an item sits in that pile, it incurs a cost:
 
- Tied-up capital: An item sitting in a box cannot be sold. It represents locked working capital that could be funding your next purchase order.
- Depreciation and seasonality: If a winter coat is returned in late February but isn&#x27;t processed until mid-March, you may miss the window to sell it at full price, forcing a markdown.
- Inventory holding costs: You are paying warehouse storage fees for stock that is not actively generating revenue. 

## How to Optimise Your Return-to-Restock Workflow 
 To protect your margins, the goal of reverse logistics should be speed. You need to close the gap between the moment an item hits the loading dock and the moment it becomes available to buy on your storefront.
 Here are the operational steps to tighten up that workflow.

### 1. Standardise the RMA Process 
 A smooth return starts before the item even leaves the customer&#x27;s hands. Utilising a clear Return Merchandise Authorisation (RMA) process ensures your warehouse team knows exactly what is coming back and why. 
 Instead of opening an unmarked box and trying to match it to a customer email, the RMA provides a barcode or reference number that pulls up the original order data, speeding up the initial intake.

### 2. Streamline Inspection and Grading 
 Once the item is received, it needs to be inspected. Is it damaged? Is it in good condition? If your team relies on paper checklists or manual spreadsheets to log this information, the process stalls. 
 Equipping your receiving team with a standardised digital workflow allows them to grade the item and route it, either to a refurbishment bin, a liquidation pile, or straight back to the active picking shelves.

### 3. Automate the Inventory Sync 
 Once an item is approved for resale, it needs to be pushed back to your eCommerce channels. If your warehouse team has to email the eCommerce manager to manually update Shopify at the end of the week, you lose days of sales potential.

## Recovering Capital Faster 
 Efficient eCommerce returns management is a cash flow strategy. By treating reverse logistics with the same urgency as outbound fulfilment, you ensure that returned items spend less time gathering dust and more time generating revenue.
 Moving away from manual spreadsheets and adopting a structured RMA workflow allows you to recover your capital faster, protect your margins, and resolve customer issues efficiently.
