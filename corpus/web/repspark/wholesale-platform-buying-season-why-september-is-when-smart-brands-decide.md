# Wholesale Platform Buying Season, Why September Is When Smart Brands Decide 

 
 
 
 by Sawyer Frank on August 26, 2026
 

 
 Every year the same thing happens. A brand decides in November that its ordering process cannot survive another season. Then it spends January watching buyers wait while a new platform is still being configured.

 The decision was not wrong. The timing was.

## September is the only month the calendar gives you

 September is the last quiet room in the wholesale year. Fall shipments are moving without much help from you. Spring line development is underway but not locked. Your reps are busy but not buried. Next year's budget is still a conversation rather than a closed file.

 That combination does not come back. In October the numbers get real. In November nobody wants to touch the systems that take orders. In December you are closing the year and half your team is out. By January you are selling with whatever you already have, and you are living with that choice for two full seasons.

 So the question is not whether your ordering process needs to change. If you are reading this, you already know it does. The question is whether you decide while there is still runway, or after the runway is gone.

## If you sell into golf, the deadline has a date on it

 PGA Show runs January 26-29, 2027 at the Orange County Convention Center in Orlando, with Demo Day on January 26. That is roughly five months from now.

 Five months sounds like plenty. Look at what has to be true before you write your first order in Orlando.

 Product data that matches the line you are actually showing. Pricing and terms loaded by account type. Spring assortments built and segmented. Images your buyers can shop rather than squint at. Inventory numbers your reps can quote out loud without hedging. A login a green grass buyer can figure out while standing at your booth with three other appointments waiting.

 None of that gets built in the last two weeks of December. Every piece of it depends on people inside your company who already have full jobs.

 Then there is the part most brands forget. Your retailers have to be onboarded too. A platform your buyers have never logged into is not a platform, it is a demo. The brands who leave Orlando with a full order book are the ones whose accounts already knew where the catalog lived before they walked the floor.

 Show season is also where the cost of the old way is most visible. A buyer commits at the booth, the order gets written on paper or in a spreadsheet, and then it sits for a week waiting on someone to key it in. Somewhere in that week the size run sells through, the terms get questioned, and a commitment turns into a conversation. Multiply that by a few hundred appointments.

## Be honest about your own complexity

 Here is the part a vendor will not always volunteer, and you should ask about it directly.

 If you are a smaller or mid sized brand with clean product data and a straightforward setup, a September decision comfortably clears a January launch. If you are running a deep ERP integration, multiple divisions, licensed product, or global entities, that is a longer build. Enterprise implementations with real integration complexity are typically measured in quarters, not weeks. Which is exactly the argument for starting the conversation in September instead of November, because in that scenario a January go live was already a summer decision, and the honest answer might be a spring launch done properly rather than a January launch done badly.

 Either way, you learn that in a scoping conversation, not in a proposal you read in December.

## Ask these five questions before you sign anything

 What is my go live date in writing, and what does it depend on. Any vendor can say yes. Ask which of the dependencies sit on your side of the table.

 Who does the product data work. This is where most implementations actually slow down. Get a named owner.

 How do my retailers get onboarded, and how long does it take them. Adoption is the whole return. A platform your accounts avoid is a line item, not an asset.

 What happens when a rep is standing in a pro shop with no signal. Wholesale does not happen at a desk.

 What does support look like the week after we launch, not the month we sign. Ask what the first thirty days include.

 If a vendor gets uncomfortable with any of those, that is your answer.

## What this looks like when it works

 Golf brands have already run this play.

 Straight Down launched more than 70 microsites in 2023 and averaged more than $11,000 in revenue per event. As Robert Ogden put it, "Microsites allowed us to keep serving customers even when golf shops were closed."

 Primo Golf came in as a direct to consumer brand suddenly buried in wholesale requests handled by email, with no real time inventory visibility. Wholesale revenue grew 70 percent in the first year on the platform. David Timmins described it plainly. "RepSpark makes the buying process of wholesale ordering very simple and easy as you can see live inventory numbers, it has completely streamlined our workflow."

 Nexbelt moved close to 100 reps off Excel and an aging platform and cut order processing time by 80 percent, from ten minutes to one. Seth Halliwill said, "I don't have to talk to a co-worker to know when things are arriving. I just look it up in RepSpark."

 Turtleson used RepSpark Community to reach more than 40,000 retailers, opened 200 new shopping relationships, and generated more than $114,000 in Community driven revenue. Andy Plate, President, said, "We felt like we had to be in the Community to be relevant."

 None of those brands got there by deciding in December.

## The window is open right now

 You have about five months until Orlando. Four if you want your retailers trained before the show rather than during it.

 The brands who will be taking clean, complete orders at PGA Show in January are having their first conversation this month. That is not urgency for its own sake, it is just how the calendar works.
