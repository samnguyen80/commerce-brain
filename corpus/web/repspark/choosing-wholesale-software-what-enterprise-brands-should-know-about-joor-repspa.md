# Choosing Wholesale Software: What Enterprise Brands Should Know About JOOR, RepSpark and NuORDER 

 
 
 
 by Tim McLain on August 26, 2026
 

 
 If you're an enterprise brand running a wholesale business today, there's a good chance you're already using more than one system to support it. That's not inefficiency, it's usually a sign that your buyers aren't all the same. A department store merchant working a linesheet six months out, a boutique owner discovering your brand at a trade show, and a specialty retailer placing a small reorder the same week they run out of stock are three different buying behaviors. Increasingly, the software market has responded by building platforms around those specific behaviors rather than trying to serve all of them equally well.

 Three names come up most often in these conversations: JOOR, RepSpark, and NuORDER. Brands evaluating their wholesale tech stack often start by asking which one is "best," but that framing usually leads to the wrong conclusion. A more useful question is which buyer each platform was actually built around and whether your current stack has a gap.

## They solve overlapping but distinct problems

 All three platforms do the same basic job at a high level: they let brands present product to retail buyers, take orders, and sync that activity back to an ERP. But the buyer experience, retailer network, and channel focus differ meaningfully.

 NuORDER is built around structured, planned wholesale ordering. The kind of workflow a national account or major chain buyer uses when working a season's linesheet. It's commonly used by enterprise brands to manage relationships with large, established retail partners (think Nordstrom's) where order volume is high and the buying cycle is predictable.

 JOOR operates more like a global marketplace layered on top of order management. It's known for its scale. Industry reporting puts its network in the range of 14,000+ brands and several hundred thousand retail buyers across 150 countries, with particular strength among luxury and fashion brands. JOOR also invests heavily in retailer discovery and trade-show-style buying experiences, including a digital trade show portal, which makes it a strong fit for brands trying to expand their retailer base globally rather than simply service existing accounts.

 RepSpark is generally positioned around independent and specialty retail. Smaller accounts that reorder frequently, need faster turnaround, and often require add-ons like decoration or custom cresting. It has a particularly strong footprint in golf and team dealer channels, where a large share of specialty retailers are reportedly already using the platform to buy from other brands, according to industry survey data cited by the company.

## The architecture question matters more than the feature list

 For an enterprise brand, the more important question usually isn't which platform has the better UI it's how each one fits into an existing systems landscape. Across all three platforms, the common pattern is the same: the brand's ERP remains the system of record, and the wholesale platform acts as a connected front end that syncs orders and inventory back and forth, typically via API or file-based integration. None of the three platforms position themselves as a replacement for ERP-level data.

 That architecture is worth understanding because it changes the nature of the decision. Adopting a second or third wholesale platform isn't necessarily a migration it can be closer to adding another consumer of a data feed your systems already produce. That's a meaningfully different project than switching from one platform to another, and it's part of why many enterprise brands run more than one of these tools at once rather than treating the choice as exclusive.

## Why brands often end up running more than one

 It's increasingly common for a large brand to run EDI for its biggest chain accounts, a linesheet platform for planned national account buying, and a separate tool for the long tail of specialty and independent retailers — because a single platform rarely serves all three buying behaviors equally well. The same logic tends to apply when comparing JOOR, RepSpark, and NuORDER: each is optimized for a different segment of the retailer base, and the businesses that get the most value tend to match the platform to the channel rather than trying to force one tool to do everything.

 A brand primarily selling into major department stores and chains may find NuORDER's structured workflow well-suited to that relationship. A brand focused on global expansion into luxury or fashion-forward independent boutiques may lean on JOOR's discovery tools and international network. A brand with meaningful distribution through golf pro shops, resort accounts, or other specialty retail may find that neither of those tools was built with that buyer in mind — and that a platform like RepSpark, which is more purpose-built for high-frequency, smaller-order specialty buying, closes that gap.

## Questions worth asking before evaluating any of these platforms

 Rather than starting with a vendor comparison, enterprise brands are often better served starting with an internal audit:

 
 Segment your retailer base. Which accounts are on EDI, which are managed through a national account platform, and which fall into neither category?

- Check reorder behavior in that third group. How many of those accounts are placing orders without a rep manually intervening? A low number often points to a tooling gap rather than a sales execution problem.

- Map buyer type to channel strategy. Is your growth priority protecting large chain relationships, expanding into new global boutique and luxury accounts, or building out specialty and independent distribution? Each answer points toward a different platform's strengths.
 

## Takeaways

 There isn't a single "winner" among JOOR, RepSpark, and NuORDER, because they weren't built to compete for the same buyer. The more useful exercise for an enterprise brand is treating your wholesale tech stack the way you'd treat any channel strategy: identify the distinct buying behaviors in your retailer base, and make sure each one has a platform that was actually designed for it. For many large brands, that conclusion points not to picking one of these three, but to understanding which combination best covers the full range of how their retailers actually buy.

 Vendor-reported figures (network size, transaction volume, survey statistics) cited in this piece come from each company's public materials and industry reporting, not independent verification, and should be confirmed directly with the vendor before being used in procurement decisions.
