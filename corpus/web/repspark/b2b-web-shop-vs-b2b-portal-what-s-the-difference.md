# B2B Web Shop vs. B2B Portal: What's the Difference? 

 
 
 
 by Tim McLain on August 17, 2026
 

 
 "Web shop," "storefront," "portal," "B2B ecommerce platform" — in most wholesale conversations, these terms get used as if they're interchangeable. Most of the time, nobody stops to draw a hard line between them, and for a brand just starting to sell online to retail accounts, that's understandable. But the two ends of that terminology spectrum — a simple B2B web shop and a full B2B portal — represent genuinely different levels of capability, and picking a solution that's really only a web shop when your business needs a portal is one of the more common reasons wholesale brands outgrow their platform within a year or two.

 Here's the actual distinction, not just the marketing language around it.

## What Is a B2B Web Shop?

 A B2B web shop is, at its core, a digital catalog with a cart and a checkout — an online store built for businesses to buy from, but structurally closer to a consumer ecommerce experience than a true account management system. It typically includes a product catalog, the ability to add items to a cart and check out, and maybe account-specific pricing layered on top. What it usually doesn't include is much beyond the transaction itself: no deep order history, limited or no multi-user access per account, and little in the way of credit management, approval workflows, or real operational integration with a brand's inventory and ERP systems.

 A web shop optimizes for the same thing consumer ecommerce optimizes for: making the purchase itself as frictionless as possible. That's a reasonable goal, but it's a narrower one than what most growing wholesale relationships actually require.

## What Is a B2B Portal?

 A B2B portal is built around a different premise entirely: that a retail account is an ongoing relationship, not a series of one-off transactions, and the platform should function as the digital hub for that entire relationship. A true B2B customer portal typically includes:

 
 Self-service order entry tied to real-time inventory visibility , so a buyer sees what's actually available to sell, not a static catalog that may already be outdated.

- Account-specific pricing and terms , reflecting negotiated rates, tiered pricing, or contract terms specific to that retailer.

- Multi-user access per account , since a retail account often has more than one person who needs access — a buyer, an operations contact, someone managing invoices — each potentially needing different permissions.

- Order history and reordering , letting a buyer see past orders and quickly reorder without rebuilding a cart from scratch every time.

- Approval workflows , for larger organizations where a purchase needs sign-off before it's finalized.

- Credit and accounts receivable visibility , so a buyer can see and manage invoices and payment status without a separate manual process.

- Reporting and analytics , giving both the brand and the buyer visibility into ordering patterns, not just a transaction history.

- Deeper backend integration , connecting the portal to a brand's ERP, inventory, and fulfillment systems so what a buyer sees reflects the actual state of the business, not a separately maintained product feed.
 
 In short: a web shop helps a buyer complete a purchase. A portal helps a buyer manage an entire ongoing account relationship, of which purchasing is just one part.

## Why the Terms Get Used Interchangeably

 Part of the confusion is that the industry hasn't been consistent about the language. Plenty of platforms marketed as "storefronts" or "web shops" have quietly added portal-like features over time, and plenty of true portals still use "shop" or "store" in their own branding because that's the language buyers are used to. The result is that a brand evaluating options often can't tell from the marketing copy alone whether they're looking at a genuine account management platform or a catalog-and-checkout tool with a portal-sounding name attached to it. The way to actually tell the difference is to look past the label and ask what happens after the "buy" button — is there real account history, real-time inventory, and a relationship being managed, or does the experience end at the transaction?

## Which One Does Your Brand Actually Need?

 The honest answer depends on the complexity of your wholesale business, not its size alone:

 

- If most of your retail accounts place simple, infrequent orders with straightforward pricing and a single point of contact, a web shop may genuinely be enough for now.

- If you're managing negotiated pricing across accounts, retailers with multiple buyers or departments, credit terms and invoicing, or inventory spread across multiple warehouses, you've already outgrown what a web shop is built to handle — even if it still technically "works."

- If your sales team is still manually answering questions a portal would answer automatically — "is this back in stock," "what did we order last time," "what do we owe" — that manual overhead is the clearest sign a real portal would pay for itself quickly.
 
 Most wholesale brands don't start out needing a full portal. They grow into needing one, often faster than they expect, and the brands that wait too long to make the switch tend to feel it first in sales team bandwidth and retailer frustration, not in the platform itself.

## The Bottom Line

 A B2B web shop and a B2B portal can look similar on the surface — both let a business buyer place an order online. What separates them is everything that happens around that order: pricing, inventory accuracy, account history, credit visibility, and whether multiple people at a retail account can actually use the system the way their business operates. Getting this distinction right early saves a wholesale brand from outgrowing its platform right around the time growth actually starts to matter.

 

## Frequently Asked Questions

 Q: What is the main difference between a B2B web shop and a B2B portal? A: A B2B web shop is primarily a digital catalog with a cart and checkout, focused on completing a transaction. A B2B portal is a broader account management system that includes real-time inventory, account-specific pricing, order history, multi-user access, credit and invoice visibility, and deeper integration with a brand's backend systems, built to manage an ongoing account relationship rather than just a single purchase.

 Q: Is a B2B portal always better than a B2B web shop? A: Not necessarily. A simpler web shop can be perfectly adequate for a brand with straightforward pricing, infrequent orders, and a single point of contact per retail account. A portal becomes the better fit once a brand is managing negotiated pricing, multiple users per account, credit terms, or inventory across multiple locations.

 Q: How can I tell if a platform is a real B2B portal or just a web shop with different branding? A: Look past the marketing language and ask what happens after checkout. A real portal offers account history, real-time inventory visibility, multi-user access, and integration with a brand's inventory and ERP systems. If the experience effectively ends at the transaction with no deeper account management layer, it's functioning as a web shop regardless of what it's called.

 Q: Can a brand start with a B2B web shop and move to a portal later? A: Yes, and many brands do exactly that as their wholesale business grows. The challenge is timing the switch before the limitations of a basic web shop start costing sales team time and retailer goodwill, since those signs tend to show up gradually rather than as a single obvious breaking point.

 Q: What signs indicate a brand has outgrown a basic B2B web shop? A: Common signs include a sales team manually answering questions about stock, order history, or invoice status that a portal would surface automatically, retail accounts with multiple buyers or departments needing different access levels, and inventory spread across multiple warehouses that a simple catalog can't reflect in real time.

 Q: Does a B2B portal replace the need for a sales rep? A: No. A portal automates the transactional and account-management work, such as order entry, reordering, and invoice visibility, freeing up sales reps to focus on relationship building, new account development, and strategic conversations rather than manual order processing and status updates.

 Q: Is RepSpark a B2B web shop or a B2B portal?
